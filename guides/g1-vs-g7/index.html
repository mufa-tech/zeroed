G1 vs G7 BC: which one to use

Two numbers on the box, two drag models in your app. What G1 and G7 actually are, why the G7 number is smaller, and how to pick the one that fits your bullet.

What a BC really says

A ballistic coefficient (BC) compares your bullet with a standard reference projectile. Each reference comes with a drag curve: how its drag coefficient (Cd) changes with speed, expressed as a Mach number. A point-mass solver takes that curve, scales it by your BC, and integrates the flight step by step.

So a BC only means something together with its drag model. “0.505” on its own says nothing. “0.505 G1” does.

Two reference shapes, two curves

G1 is the old standard. Its reference is a flat-base projectile with a short, blunt nose, a shape few modern rifle bullets resemble. G7 uses a long boattail projectile with a long, pointed nose, much closer to today’s long-range bullets.

Look at the shapes, not just the height. G7 has less drag overall, and its jump near the speed of sound is sharper. Both curves are the standard tables that ballistic solvers use, so the same model gives the same curve in every app.

Why the G7 number is smaller

The G7 reference already has low drag. A modern boattail bullet is much closer to it than to the G1 reference, so its BC relative to G7 is lower. For a typical long-range boattail bullet, the G7 BC is roughly half the G1 BC. That doesn’t make the bullet worse. It just means a different yardstick.

The practical rule: never compare a G1 number with a G7 number, and never type one into a field meant for the other.

Why the shape of the curve matters

If your bullet’s drag curve has the same shape as the reference curve, a single BC fits at every speed. Long boattail bullets follow the G7 shape closely, so their G7 BC stays fairly constant as they slow down. Their G1 BC drifts with velocity. That is why some makers publish several G1 BCs for different velocity bands (multi-BC): each band is a straight-line fix for a curve that doesn’t quite fit.

Flat-base bullets and many shorter hunting bullets are a different story. For them, G1 often fits well, and the G1 number is often the only one published.

Out to about 500 yd the two models agree to within a click on most scopes. At 600 yd they are about 0.1 mil apart, at 1000 yd about 0.5 mil, roughly 20 inches. The G1 version also stays supersonic farther: in this example to about 1100 yd, against about 970 yd for G7.

Which one is right? The table can’t tell you. It depends on how well each BC describes this bullet over the speeds it actually flies. Only shots at distance can answer that.

Which one to use: a short checklist

  • Use the model the BC was published in. A G7 number goes in a G7 field. Always.
  • Long-range boattail bullet with a published G7 BC? Use G7.
  • Only a G1 BC available? Use it. If the maker publishes velocity bands, enter them as multi-BC.
  • Flat-base, round-nose or many short hunting bullets: G1 is a fine fit.
  • Don’t convert G1 to G7 with a fixed factor. The ratio between them changes with velocity, so a single factor is only right at one speed.
  • Know where the number came from. Some makers state how their BCs were measured. A measured value for your exact bullet beats a generic one.
  • Whatever you choose, confirm it at distance.

Truing closes the gap

Even a good BC is an average over many bullets and lots, and your muzzle velocity reading has its own error. When your observed drop at distance disagrees with the calculation, adjust one input so the curve matches: muzzle velocity if the error shows up at mid range, BC if it only shows up far out. That is truing. After that, the drag model matters much less, because the curve now passes through your real impacts.

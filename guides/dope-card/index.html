How to make a DOPE card (and print it)

A DOPE card is the cheat sheet on your stock: range in, hold out. Here is how to build one that is actually right for your rifle, and how to print it so it survives the field.

What goes on a DOPE card

DOPE stands for “data on previous engagements”. In practice it is a table: for each distance, the elevation and a wind hold, in the units of your scope. Mil (MRAD) or MOA, or clicks if you dial. Some shooters add velocity, time of flight or energy. At the top goes everything the table depends on: rifle, load, zero distance, and the conditions it was calculated for.

A card is only as good as the numbers that went into it. So most of the work happens before you print.

Step 1: get the inputs right

  • A confirmed zero, and its distance. A 100 yd zero and a 100 m zero are not the same input. If you zero high on purpose (a hunting zero), enter the distance where bullet and line of sight meet again, not 100.
  • Your muzzle velocity, from a chronograph. The number on the box was measured in the maker’s test barrel. Yours can be faster or slower. Average several shots and note the temperature.
  • The BC with the right drag model. Take the bullet maker’s number and enter it as G1 or G7, whichever it was published in. See G1 vs G7 BC: which one to use.
  • Sight height. Center of bore to center of scope. Measure from the top of the scope tube to the bottom of the barrel at the same spot, then subtract half the tube diameter and half the barrel diameter.
  • Scope units and click value, checked. Dial 10 mil and the impact at 100 m should move 1 m. Dial 10 MOA and at 100 yd it should move about 10.5 in (1 MOA is 1.047 in at 100 yd). If your scope does not track true, the card can’t fix that.
  • Conditions. Station pressure (the real pressure where you stand, not the sea-level number from the weather report), temperature, and humidity or altitude.

Step 2: choose the range and the step

Cover the distances you actually shoot, not the maximum your app can do. A hunting card might run 100 to 400 yd every 50 yd. A match card might run 300 to 1000 yd every 50 yd. Elevation changes faster the farther out you go, so if anything use a finer step at the far end, not the near end.

Round elevation to what you can dial. With 0.1 mil clicks, a second decimal on the card is noise.

Step 3: add one wind column

Wind is the hard part, so keep it simple. Put one reference wind on the card, such as 10 mph full value (from 3 or 9 o’clock), and scale from there:

  • 5 mph is roughly half the 10 mph hold, 15 mph roughly one and a half times.
  • A wind from 1:30 or 4:30 (45°) pushes about 70% of full value.
  • A wind from 1 or 11 o’clock (30° off the line) pushes about half.

Hold or dial into the wind: for a wind from 3 o’clock, that is to the right. These rules of thumb are approximate. They are good enough for a card because your wind call will be less precise than the scaling anyway.

Notice how the elevation steps grow: 0.8 mil between 200 and 300 yd, 2.0 mil between 900 and 1000 yd. That is why a range error hurts much more far out, and why a finer step there pays off.

Step 5: verify and true

A calculated card is a starting point. Shoot it at two or three known distances, with several shots each, and write down the conditions.

  • Impacts consistently high or low at mid range usually mean the muzzle velocity is off.
  • Misses that only show up far out, especially as the bullet nears the speed of sound, point to the BC.

Correct the input (that is called truing), recalculate, and print again. Don’t hand-edit single rows: if one row is wrong, the ones around it are too.

Step 6: print it so it lasts

  • Print the PDF at 100% (“actual size”), not “fit to page”, if it has to fit a specific spot on the stock, a scope cap or a wrist coach.
  • Big type, black on white. Don’t rely on color alone; you will read it in bright sun, at dusk and with your eye half on the scope.
  • Protect it. Laminate it or cover it with clear packing tape.
  • One card per load and per set of conditions. Summer and winter, home range and a mountain hunt are different cards. Print the conditions and the date on each one.

Keep the app as the backup and the card as the plan. Paper doesn’t need a battery.
